Summary

The project asks which large US medical-device companies offer the best combination of quality, growth and value, and whether the best of them are worth buying at their prices on 6 November 2026. A screen of 17 companies on 12 metrics selected three finalists. Each was then valued with a discounted cash flow (DCF) model, a reverse DCF, peer multiples and a 10,000-run Monte Carlo simulation, and classified with a buy/hold/sell rule written before any value was computed.

The screen and the valuation answer different questions. The screen ranks companies on what they have done; the valuation asks what the share price already assumes about the future. Two of the three finalists are priced for almost exactly the growth their management guides to. The third, DexCom, is priced for growth roughly twice its guidance.

1Question and approach

All rules (eligibility, metrics, weights, valuation method and decision rule) were written down before any results were seen, so that the conclusion could not be adjusted to favour a particular company. Every later change is recorded with its reason in the decision log in the README, including two changes to the valuation method made after a sanity check (section 4).

  1. Universe. 21 candidate companies, filtered by fixed rules to 17.
  2. Data. Prices, financial statements and interest rates downloaded, saved unaltered and cross-checked against official filings.
  3. Screen. 12 metrics in five groups, converted to percentile ranks and combined with fixed weights. The ranking is then tested against 10,000 alternative weightings.
  4. Valuation. The three most robust companies are valued with a DCF, a reverse DCF and trading comparables, under bear, base and bull assumptions.
  5. Monte Carlo and decision. 10,000 DCFs with randomised assumptions give a distribution of values and the probability that each company is worth more than its price.

2Universe and data

A company is included if it is listed in the US and reports in dollars, if medical devices are its main business, if its market value exceeds $5bn, if it has at least three years of history, and if its latest year shows positive operating income and free cash flow. Of the 21 candidates, four were excluded.

Sources

Daily prices (adjusted for dividends and splits) and annual financial statements come from Yahoo Finance. Revenue and operating income for every company were compared with the figures in its own 10-K filing, taken from the SEC's XBRL data: all 34 values match within 1%. Interest rates come from FRED (the 10-year Treasury yield for the DCF and the 3-month bill for the Sharpe ratio). Company guidance comes from earnings releases.

What checking the data found

  • An intraday price recorded as a close. A download during US trading hours returned a mid-day price, on a quarter of normal volume. Unfinished trading days are now removed automatically.
  • Adjusted operating income. Yahoo's "Operating Income" excludes charges it treats as one-off; for Stryker such charges occurred in every year from 2022 to 2025. The as-reported figure, which matches the 10-K, is used throughout.
  • Restated revenue. Teleflex re-reported 2023–25 revenue without the businesses it is selling, but not 2022, so its 3-year growth compared two different companies. Restatements are now detected automatically, and growth is measured over the longest comparable period.
  • A spin-off recorded as a stock split. Becton Dickinson's distribution of Waters shares appears in the price history as a 1.272-for-1 split. The factor was checked by hand: 207.40 ÷ (207.40 − 44.30) = 1.272.
  • A missing debt line. Intuitive Surgical shows no debt in Yahoo's data. Its 10-K confirms it has none, and the value is recorded with the filing reference.
  • A rate that changed with the run date. FRED publishes the Treasury yield one day late, so the latest value changed between runs (4.90%, then 4.84%). The rate date is fixed at 5 November 2026 so that results do not depend on when the code is run.

3The screen

Each of the 12 metrics is converted to a percentile rank: the share of the other 16 companies that a company beats, from 0 (worst) to 100 (best). For metrics where lower is better, such as debt or volatility, the order is reversed. The ranks are averaged within five groups, which are combined with weights of 30% Quality, 25% Growth, 20% Value, 15% Momentum and 10% Risk. Percentile ranks prevent a single extreme value from dominating the score: Insulet's operating profit, for example, grew 133% a year from a very small base.

Select a row to see a company's individual metrics.

How much does the ranking depend on the weights?

The weights are a judgement, so the ranking was recomputed 10,000 times with each group's weight drawn at random within ±10 percentage points of its chosen value and rescaled to sum to 100%. Figure 1 shows the range of ranks each company obtains. The finalists are the three companies with the highest probability of a top-three finish.

Risk and risk-adjusted return

Volatility measures how much a share price swings: the standard deviation of daily returns, scaled to a year (multiplied by √252). The Sharpe ratio measures how much return a share delivered for each unit of that risk: its yearly return minus the risk-free rate (the average 3-month Treasury bill, about 4.3%), divided by its volatility. A Sharpe ratio below zero means the share did worse than a risk-free deposit over the period.

Volatility is one of the 12 scored metrics (Risk group); the Sharpe ratio is reported but not scored, because it mixes return and risk, which the screen already measures separately. Over these three years no device company came close to the S&P 500's risk-adjusted return. The sector as a whole lagged the market: $100 invested in the device-sector fund became about $124, against about $175 in the S&P 500.

Risk over time

The figures above summarise three years in one number. Measured over a moving 12-month window instead, volatility and the Sharpe ratio show when risk rose or fell: at each date, they are calculated from the previous 12 months only. The curves therefore start in November 2024, one year after the price series.

A moving window has a side effect worth knowing: a single extreme day stays in the 12-month figures for exactly one year and then drops out all at once. DexCom fell 40.7% on 26 July 2024; its 12-month volatility stayed high until 30 July 2025, when that day left the window and the figure fell by 17 points in a day. 4Valuation

Discounted cash flow. Revenue is projected for ten years. Year-1 growth comes from the company's guidance and declines in equal steps to 2.5% by year 11. The EBITDA margin moves from today's level to a target, and depreciation converges to capital expenditure. Free cash flow equals operating profit after tax, plus depreciation, minus capital expenditure and the increase in working capital. Cash flows are discounted at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), and a terminal value captures all years after year 10.

Discount rate. The cost of equity follows the capital asset pricing model: the 10-year Treasury yield (4.84%) plus an adjusted beta times a 5% equity risk premium. The cost of debt is the Treasury yield plus 1%, after tax. The two are weighted by market value.

Reverse DCF. The model is solved for the year-1 growth at which the value equals the share price, i.e. the growth the market is paying for.

Comparables. The EV/EBITDA and EV/Sales multiples of four or five similar companies are applied to the company's own EBITDA and sales.

Changes after a sanity check. The first version of the model (5-year forecast, operating-margin based, balance-sheet working capital) valued every company below any multiple observed in the market, and modelled DexCom's free cash flow at 9.6% of revenue against 23.1% actually earned. The forecast was extended to ten years, the margin was defined on EBITDA so that non-cash amortisation of past acquisitions is treated correctly, and working capital was taken from the cash flow statement. The first run, which gave SELL for all three companies, is kept in the repository.

5Monte Carlo simulation

A single DCF gives one value from one set of assumptions. The simulation values the company 10,000 times instead. In each run, year-1 growth, the target EBITDA margin and the WACC are drawn from triangular distributions between the bear and bull cases, with the base case as the most likely value, and long-run growth is drawn uniformly between 2.0% and 3.0%. The result is a distribution of values per share, from which the median and the probability of the value exceeding the price are read.

The settings start at the model's own assumptions and can be changed to see how the conclusion responds; for example, how much higher growth would have to be for DexCom's distribution to reach its price.

6Conclusions and limitations

Globus Medical (HOLD). It ranked first in the screen because of 42% annual revenue growth at the lowest EV/EBITDA multiple in the universe. Most of that growth came from acquisitions (NuVasive in 2023 and later Nevro). The business is guided to grow about 5% in 2026, and the price implies 5.1%. It trades at a discount to its peers, but its current growth explains the discount.

ResMed (HOLD). It combines high returns on capital and strong cash generation with no weak group in the screen. The price implies 7.1% growth against 7% core growth in the latest quarter, so the valuation is consistent with recent performance.

DexCom (SELL). It scores highest on quality, but the price implies roughly 28% growth next year and 16% a year for ten years, against guidance of 12–13%. All methods give values below the price. The result holds when share-based pay is added back, capital expenditure is reduced to depreciation and the WACC is lowered to 8.5%, all at the same time.

Limitations

  • Survivorship and takeover bias. Three of the 20 candidates were acquired or are being acquired, and acquired companies are often among the most attractive.
  • Acquired and organic growth cannot be separated with the data used; Globus Medical shows why this matters.
  • Assumptions. The equity risk premium, terminal growth and beta adjustment are conventional choices rather than measured facts. The simulation varies the most influential assumptions, but not all of them.
  • Guidance. The base cases rely on company guidance. A company that regularly exceeds its guidance would be undervalued by this approach.
  • Model probabilities. P(value > price) is the share of simulated futures above the price given the model and the ranges chosen. It shows how robust the conclusion is to those assumptions; it is not the real-world probability that the share is undervalued.
  • Independent draws. The simulation draws growth, margin and the WACC independently, although in reality they are related.
  • Robustness over weights only. The 10,000-run ranking test varies the group weights, not the choice of metrics.
  • Short history. Three years of prices may not cover a full market cycle.
